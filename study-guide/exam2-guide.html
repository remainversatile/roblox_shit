Pick the right formula

Source: Formula sheet; Chapter 6–8 slides

First decide what the question is about: one value, an average, or a proportion. Then decide what it wants: a probability, an interval, or a sample size.

The problem gives or asks…Use
Probability for one individual value x\(z=\dfrac{x-\mu}{\sigma}\)
Cutoff value x from a percentileFind z from the LEFT area, then \(\mathrm{x}=\mu+(\mathrm{z})(\sigma)\)
Unknown mean from x and a percentile\(\mu=\mathrm{x}-(\mathrm{z})(\sigma)\)
Probability about a sample mean \(\bar{x}\)\(\sigma_{\bar{X}}=\dfrac{\sigma}{\sqrt{n}}\), then \(z_{\bar{x}}=\dfrac{\bar{x}-\mu}{\sigma_{\bar{x}}}\)
Probability about a sample proportion \(\bar{p}\)\(\sigma_{\bar{p}}=\sqrt{\dfrac{p(1-p)}{n}}\), then \(z_{\bar{p}}=\dfrac{\bar{p}-p}{\sigma_{\bar{p}}}\)
CI for a mean, σ given\(\mu=\bar{x}\pm\left(Z_{\alpha/2}\right)\left(\dfrac{\sigma}{\sqrt{n}}\right)\)
CI for a mean, only s given\(\mu=\bar{x}\pm\left(t_{a/2}\right)\left(\dfrac{s}{\sqrt{n}}\right)\), df = n − 1
CI for a population proportion\(p=\bar{p}\pm\left(Z_{\alpha/2}\right)\sqrt{\dfrac{\bar{p}(1-\bar{p})}{\mathrm{n}}}\)
Minimum n to estimate a mean\(\mathrm{n}=\left(\dfrac{(z_{\alpha/2})\sigma}{E}\right)^{2}\), round UP
Minimum n to estimate a proportion\(n=\dfrac{\left(z_{\alpha/2}\right)^{2}p^{*}(1-p^{*})}{E^{2}}\), round UP

Three decisions that prevent most mistakes

  1. One value or an average? A sample mean varies by \(\sigma/\sqrt{n}\), not by σ. A story that mentions 50 people may still ask about just one person, so identify the actual random variable.
  2. σ or s? For a mean CI, use t whenever the population σ is unknown, even if n > 30. Practice Test 2 uses t at n = 60 and n = 32. A large sample does not turn s into σ.
  3. Known p or estimated \(\bar{p}\)? In a Chapter 7 probability, use the given population p in \(\sigma_{\bar{p}}\). In a Chapter 8 CI, p is unknown, so use \(\bar{p}\). For sample-size planning, use p* from a pilot study, or .50 when there isn't one.

Chapter 6 · Normal curves: values → areas

Source: DS Chapter 6 Normal Curve slides 3–24 · Chapter 6 PDF pp. 1–19 · Practice Test 2 goldfish problem

Facts to know without calculating

  • A normal distribution is continuous, bell-shaped, symmetric, and described by μ and σ. Mean = median = mode.
  • The total area is 1, and each side of the mean has area .50. The standard normal distribution has mean 0 and standard deviation 1.
  • A positive z is above the mean and a negative z is below it.
  • For a continuous variable, P(X = a) = 0, so P(X < a) = P(X ≤ a).
  • Empirical rule: about 68%, 95%, and 99.7% of values fall within 1, 2, and 3σ of the mean. For an exact central 95%, use 1.96 rather than 2.

The course z-table gives the area to the LEFT

Compute \(z=\dfrac{x-\mu}{\sigma}\) and round to two decimals. The row gives the ones and tenths digits, and the column gives the hundredths. Example: row 1.2, column .04 → .8925 for z = 1.24.

Asked forUse the cumulative table this way
Less than / below aLEFT area at z(a)
Greater than / above a1 − LEFT area at z(a)
Between a and bLEFT area at z(b) − LEFT area at z(a)
Outside a to b1 − (probability between a and b)

Course example: goldfish lifetimes, μ = 60 days, σ = 20 days

  • More than 60 days: z = (60 − 60)/20 = 0, so P = .50.
  • More than 80 days: z = (80 − 60)/20 = 1.00. LEFT area = .8413, so the right area = 1 − .8413 = .1587 (15.87%).
  • Between 25 and 45 days: z(25) = −1.75 → .0401, and z(45) = −.75 → .2266. Then .2266 − .0401 = .1865.
  • Replaced within 7 days: z = (7 − 60)/20 = −2.65 → .0040, which is .40%. A probability and a percentage differ by a factor of 100.

Sanity checks. A tail beyond the mean must be less than .50, and a far tail must be small. Every probability is between 0 and 1. To find an area between two values, subtract the areas, never the z-scores.

Chapter 6 · Percentiles: areas → values

Source: Chapter 6 slide 24 · Chapter 6 PDF pp. 18–19 · Practice Test 2

To find a cutoff from a percentage, work backwards. Convert the percentage to an area to the LEFT, find that area inside the z-table, read off z, and convert back:

\[\mathrm{x}=\mu+(\mathrm{z})(\sigma)\qquad\qquad \mu=\mathrm{x}-(\mathrm{z})(\sigma)\]
WordingArea to find in the table
Bottom 20% / 20th percentile.20
Top 10% / longest-lived 10%1 − .10 = .90
67.72% are larger than x1 − .6772 = .3228
x is larger than 86.65% of values.8665
Central 95%, symmetric.0250 and .9750

Course examples

  • Goldfish, top 10%: area .90 → z ≈ 1.28. x = 60 + (1.28)(20) = 85.6 days.
  • Goldfish, bottom 20%: area .20 → z ≈ −.84. x = 60 + (−.84)(20) = 43.2 days.
  • Goldfish, central 95%: z = ±1.96, so 60 ± 1.96(20) = 60 ± 39.20 → 20.80 to 99.20 days.
  • Unknown mean: x = 449, σ = 36, and 86.65% of values are below x. Area .8665 → z = 1.11. μ = 449 − (1.11)(36) = 409.04.
  • Unknown x: μ = 3.58, σ = 1.04, and 67.72% are larger than x. LEFT area .3228 → z = −.46. x = 3.58 + (−.46)(1.04) = 3.1016 (3.10).

Self-check. “Top 10%” means you look up .90, because the table gives the area below the cutoff. The central 95% of individual lifetimes uses σ = 20. A 95% CI for a mean uses a standard error instead. Both use 1.96, but they answer different questions.

Chapter 7 · Sampling distribution of \(\bar{x}\)

Source: DS Chapter 7 slides 8–29 · Chapter 7 PDF pp. 8–13 · Mean Sample Problems handout · Practice Test 2 credit-card problem

When asked for “the sampling distribution,” state all three

  • Center: \(E\,(\,\bar{x}\,)=\mu_{\bar{X}}=\mu\). \(\bar{x}\) is an unbiased estimator of μ.
  • Spread: \(\sigma_{\bar{X}}=\dfrac{\sigma}{\sqrt{n}}\), the standard deviation of sample means over repeated samples of size n.
  • Shape: normal for any n if the population is normal. Otherwise approximately normal when n ≥ 30 (Central Limit Theorem). The population itself does not become normal.

Sampling error is the natural difference between \(\bar{x}\) and μ from one random sample to the next. It does not mean someone made a mistake.

Course example: credit-card balances

μ = $2,864, σ = $775, n = 50. Because n ≥ 30, \(\bar{x}\) is approximately normal with center $2,864 and \(\sigma_{\bar{X}}=\dfrac{775}{\sqrt{50}}=109.6016\).

  • P(\(\bar{x}\) > $3,000): \(z_{\bar{x}}=\dfrac{3000-2864}{109.6016}=1.24\). LEFT area = .8925, so 1 − .8925 = .1075.
  • P(within $100 of μ): $2,764 ≤ \(\bar{x}\) ≤ $2,964 → z = ±.91. Then .8186 − .1814 = .6372.
  • Same question with n = 100: \(\sigma_{\bar{X}}\) = 775/10 = 77.5 → z = ±1.29. Then .9015 − .0985 = .8030.

What changes when n changes

A larger n leaves the center at μ and shrinks \(\sigma_{\bar{X}}\), so more sample means fall close to μ. Multiplying n by 4 cuts the SE in half. The population σ does not change.

Common mistake. Don't divide by 775 in the sample-mean z-score. That describes individual balances, not the mean of 50 balances. The finite-population correction factor appears in the Chapter 7 PDF, but it is not on the formula sheet and the practice handouts leave it out.

Chapter 7 · Sampling distribution of \(\bar{p}\)

Source: DS Chapter 7 slides 39–44 · Chapter 7 PDF pp. 14–16 · Proportion Sample Problems handout · Practice Test 2 internet problem

p is the population proportion and \(\bar{p}=\dfrac{x}{n}\) is the sample proportion, where x is the number of items with the characteristic. Keep proportions as decimals: 66% = .66.

  • Center: \(E\,(\,\bar{p}\,)=p.\)
  • Spread: \(\sigma_{\bar{p}}=\sqrt{\dfrac{p(1-p)}{n}}\)
  • Shape: approximately normal when \(\mathrm{np}\geq 5,\ \text{and}\ \mathrm{n}(1-\mathrm{p})\geq 5\). The Chapter 7 slides write “> 5”, but follow the formula sheet for a boundary case.
  • Probability: \(z_{\bar{p}}=\dfrac{\bar{p}-p}{\sigma_{\bar{p}}}\), then use the same table rules as in Chapter 6.

Course example: internet use among adults over 70

p = .66, n = 300. Find the probability that \(\bar{p}\) is within .04 of p. Round proportions to two decimals and the SE to four.

  1. Shape: np = 300(.66) = 198 and n(1 − p) = 300(.34) = 102. Both are ≥ 5, so the distribution is approximately normal.
  2. Center: \(E\,(\,\bar{p}\,)\) = .66
  3. Spread: \(\sigma_{\bar{p}}=\sqrt{\dfrac{(.66)(.34)}{300}}=\sqrt{.000748}\) = .0273
  4. Translate: “within .04” means .62 ≤ \(\bar{p}\) ≤ .70.
  5. Standardize: z = (.62 − .66)/.0273 = −1.47 and z = (.70 − .66)/.0273 = 1.47, giving areas .0708 and .9292.
  6. Subtract: .9292 − .0708 = .8584 (85.84%)

Traps. The n ≥ 30 rule is for means. For proportions, check both counts. If the question gives a count cutoff, convert it to \(\bar{p}\) = x/n first. Write .04, not 4.

Chapter 8 · Confidence intervals and critical values

Source: DS Chapter 8 slides 9–56 · Chapter 8 PDF pp. 1–8, 15–16 · Sigma Known / Minimum Sample Sizes handout · Practice Test 2

Every interval has the same structure: point estimate ± margin of error, where E = (critical value)(standard error). The width of the interval is 2E. A mean CI estimates μ, and a proportion CI estimates p.

For confidence level C, α = 1 − C. Split α evenly between the two tails. The critical probabilities are

\[\frac{\alpha}{2}\quad\&\quad 1-\frac{\alpha}{2}\]

Look up \(1-\frac{\alpha}{2}\) inside the z-table to get \(Z_{\alpha/2}\).

Confidenceαα/21 − α/2\(Z_{\alpha/2}\)

90%.10.0500.95001.645
95%.05.0250.97501.96
98%.02.0100.99002.33
99%.01.0050.99502.576

When a question asks for both cutoffs, give them as ±z. Use the positive value to compute E.

σ known: the Verizon problem

\(\bar{x}\) = 153 monthly minutes per residential user, σ = 46, n = 85, 95% confidence.

\[\mu=\bar{x}\pm\left(Z_{\alpha/2}\right)\left(\frac{\sigma}{\sqrt{n}}\right)=153\pm(1.96)\left(\frac{46}{\sqrt{85}}\right)=153\pm(1.96)(4.9894)=153\pm 9.7792\]

CI = (143.22, 162.78) minutes. Interpretation: We are 95% confident that the population mean monthly minutes per residential user is between 143.22 and 162.78.

Minimum n for a mean: bread expenditure

90% confidence, σ = $4, E = $1.

\[\mathrm{n}=\left(\frac{(z_{\alpha/2})\sigma}{E}\right)^{2}=\left(\frac{(1.645)(4)}{1}\right)^{2}=43.2964\]

Round up to 44 families. If σ is unknown at the planning stage, the slides use a pilot study or range/4 as an estimate.

What “95% confident” means. If you repeated the sampling many times, about 95% of the intervals built this way would contain the fixed population parameter. The interval estimates μ. It is not the range that holds 95% of individual values.

Chapter 8 · Mean CI when σ is unknown (t)

Source: DS Chapter 8 slides 57–67 · Chapter 8 PDF pp. 8–12 · Sigma Unknown t handout · Practice Test 2

\[S_{\bar{X}}=\frac{s}{\sqrt{n}}\qquad\qquad \mu=\bar{x}\pm\left(t_{a/2}\right)\left(\frac{s}{\sqrt{n}}\right),\quad df=n-1\]

For a small sample, look for the statement that the population is normal. The course allows the large-sample approach when the population isn't normal.

Reading the t-table

Go to the df row and the upper-tail area α/2 column. The number where they meet is t. The body of the t-table holds critical values, while the body of the z-table holds probabilities.

  • Upper-tail area .025, df = 12 → t = 2.179
  • Lower-tail area .05, n = 51 (df = 50) → t = −1.676
  • Central 90%, n = 26 (df = 25) → −1.708 and +1.708

Aerospace managers: the full course example

The population is normal. n = 18, \(\bar{x}\) = 13.56 overtime hours/week, s = 7.8, 90% confidence. σ is unknown → t, α = .10, α/2 = .05, df = 17 → t = 1.740.

\[\mu=13.56\pm(1.74)\left(\frac{7.8}{\sqrt{18}}\right)=13.56\pm(1.74)(1.83848)=13.56\pm 3.19895\]

CI = (10.36, 16.76) hours. We are 90% confident that the population mean weekly overtime for aerospace managers is between 10.36 and 16.76 hours.

Large samples still use t

  • \(\bar{x}\) = 25, s = 3.5, n = 60, 95%: df = 59, t = 2.001, SE = .45185, E = .90415 → (24.096, 25.904)
  • \(\bar{x}\) = 3.419, s = .974, n = 32, 90%: df = 31, t = 1.696, E = .29202 → (3.127, 3.711)

t compared with z: t is symmetric and bell-shaped but has heavier tails. At the same confidence level its critical value is larger, so the interval is wider. As df grows, t approaches z.

Chapter 8 · Proportion CI and minimum sample size

Source: DS Chapter 8 slides 68–80 · Chapter 8 PDF pp. 13–18 · Proportions / Minimum Sample Size handout · Practice Test 2

\[p=\bar{p}\pm\left(Z_{\alpha/2}\right)\sqrt{\frac{\bar{p}\left(1-\bar{p}\right)}{\mathrm{n}}}\]

Find \(\bar{p}=\dfrac{x}{n}\) if it isn't given. Check that n\(\bar{p}\) and n(1 − \(\bar{p}\)) are both at least 5.

Temporary-hire companies (Practice Test 2)

\(\bar{p}\) = .39, n = 87, 95% confidence. Counts: 87(.39) = 33.93 and 87(.61) = 53.07, both above 5.

\[p=.39\pm(1.96)\sqrt{\frac{(.39)(.61)}{87}}=.39\pm(1.96)(.0523)=.39\pm .1025\]

CI ≈ (.2875, .4925), or (.29, .49) to two decimals. We are 95% confident that the population proportion of companies using temporary hires during the holiday season is between about 28.75% and 49.25%.

Minimum n for a proportion

\[n=\frac{\left(z_{\alpha/2}\right)^{2}\,p^{*}\left(1-p^{*}\right)}{E^{2}}\]

Use a pilot p* if one is given. Otherwise use p* = .50, because .50(.50) = .25 is the largest possible p*(1 − p*) and gives the most conservative n.

  • Acme, with pilot: 98% → \(z_{\alpha/2}\) = 2.33, E = .03, p* = .40. n = (2.33)²(.40)(.60)/(.03)² = 1.302936/.0009 = 1447.71 → 1,448
  • Acme, no pilot: n = (2.33)²(.50)(.50)/(.03)² = 1.357225/.0009 = 1508.03 → 1,509. The key prints 1,508 because it rounded too early, and the round-up rule gives 1,509.

How the pieces move

ChangeEffect
Higher confidenceLarger z or t, larger E, wider interval
Larger nSmaller SE, smaller E, narrower interval
Smaller target ELarger required n. Halving E needs 4× the n
Larger σLarger required n for a mean

Units and rounding. “Within 3 percentage points” means E = .03, not 3. A minimum sample size always rounds up to the next whole number.

Concepts and vocabulary

Source: Chapter 7 PDF pp. 1–8 · Chapter 7–8 slides · Chapter 6 PDF pp. 1–2, 20

TermMeaning
Population / sampleAll elements of interest / the subset studied
ParameterDescribes the population: μ, σ, p
StatisticDescribes the sample: \(\bar{x}\), s, \(\bar{p}\)
Sampling distributionDistribution of a statistic over all possible samples of the same size
Standard errorStandard deviation of a sampling distribution

Sampling methods

MethodHow to recognize it
Simple randomEvery item in the frame has an equal chance of selection
Stratified randomDivide into non-overlapping subgroups (strata) and randomly sample from each one
SystematicRandom start, then every kth item, where k = N/n
ClusterRandomly select some groups or areas that represent the population
ConvenienceWhoever or whatever is easiest to reach
JudgmentThe researcher picks people judged to be representative
QuotaFill subgroup targets using nonrandom selection

Stratified vs. cluster: stratified sampling draws from every stratum, while cluster sampling selects only some clusters. Stratified vs. quota: stratified selection within groups is random, and quota selection is not.

Four survey errors

  • Coverage/selection: the frame leaves out relevant groups.
  • Nonresponse: people who don't answer differ from those who do.
  • Measurement: question wording, respondent mistakes, or interviewer effects distort the answers.
  • Sampling: estimates naturally vary from sample to sample.

Reasons to sample include saving time and money, and avoiding the loss of product in destructive testing.

Standard deviation from raw data

Compute the mean, each deviation, each squared deviation, and their sum. Then divide by N for a population or n − 1 for a sample, and take the square root (see sheet page 1). A normal probability plot whose points lie close to the reference line supports approximate normality.

Rounding

Professor Spencer distinguishes “round final answer only” (keep full precision until the end) from “round all calculations” (round at every step). Follow the exact number of decimals each question asks for. Canvas may reject an answer that is rounded differently. Minimum sample sizes always round up.
